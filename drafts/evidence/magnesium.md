## Level 1: human meta-analyses and RCTs

### Sleep in older adults with insomnia

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · limited or early evidence_

A meta-analysis found 3 randomized trials comparing oral magnesium with placebo in 151 older adults. People taking magnesium fell asleep 17.36 minutes faster than with placebo. Total sleep time rose by 16.06 minutes, but that was not statistically significant. The authors rated the trials at moderate-to-high risk of bias, called the evidence low to very low quality, and said it is not good enough for doctors to make well-informed recommendations.

### What it does in the body

_section: What magnesium is · reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet describes magnesium as a cofactor in more than 300 enzyme systems, including ones for protein synthesis, muscle and nerve function, blood sugar control and blood pressure. It is also needed for energy production, bone structure, and making DNA and RNA.

Sources: [1]

### Forms and absorption

_section: What magnesium is · reviewed_

According to the fact sheet, small studies found magnesium aspartate, citrate, lactate and chloride are absorbed more completely than magnesium oxide and sulfate. The fact sheet doesn’t discuss glycinate. This site compares products by the elemental magnesium on the Supplement Facts panel, not the weight of the whole compound.

Sources: [1]

### Blood pressure: a small drop

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH fact sheet says trials suggest magnesium only marginally lowers blood pressure. A Cochrane review of 12 trials (545 people with high blood pressure) found diastolic pressure fell by 2.2 mmHg. A meta-analysis of 22 studies (1,173 adults) found systolic pressure fell 3 to 4 mmHg and diastolic 2 to 3 mmHg. The FDA calls the evidence for a blood pressure claim inconsistent and inconclusive.
- Systolic: 3–4 mmHg lower (22 studies)
- Diastolic: 2–3 mmHg lower (22 studies)

Sources: [1]

### Migraine prevention

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · limited or early evidence_

Three of four small, short placebo-controlled trials found modest reductions in how often migraines happened with up to 600 mg of magnesium a day. A guideline cited by the fact sheet says magnesium is probably effective for preventing migraine.

Sources: [1]

### Blood sugar in diabetes

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · not supported_

Trial results are mixed, and the fact sheet reports the American Diabetes Association’s view that there isn’t enough evidence to support routine magnesium for blood sugar control in people with diabetes.

Sources: [1]

### Recommended intake

_section: How much magnesium · reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet lists a Recommended Dietary Allowance of 400 to 420 mg a day for adult men and 310 to 320 mg for adult women, from all sources combined.
- Adult men: 400–420 mg/day
- Adult women: 310–320 mg/day

Sources: [1]

### Upper limit for supplements

_section: How much magnesium · reviewed_

The upper limit for adults is 350 mg a day of magnesium from supplements and medications. It does not include magnesium in food. Above it, supplements can cause diarrhea, nausea and abdominal cramping.

Sources: [1]

### Too much, and kidney problems

_section: Safety · reviewed_

The fact sheet says very high doses can cause magnesium toxicity, with low blood pressure, vomiting, trouble breathing, irregular heartbeat and, rarely, cardiac arrest. The risk rises with reduced kidney function or kidney failure, because the body can’t clear the extra magnesium. Toxicity has been linked to laxative or antacid doses, typically over 5,000 mg a day.

Sources: [1]

### Medicines

_section: Safety · reviewed_

The fact sheet lists interactions with osteoporosis drugs (bisphosphonates) and some antibiotics (tetracyclines and quinolones), which should be taken hours apart from magnesium; diuretics, which can raise or lower magnesium; and long-term use of acid-reducing proton pump inhibitors, which can cause low magnesium.

Sources: [1]
