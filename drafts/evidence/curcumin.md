### A spice, and the compounds in it

_section: What turmeric and curcumin are · not reviewed_

The NIH page describes turmeric as a plant in the ginger family whose underground stem is used as a spice. Curcumin gives it its yellow color, and the effects attributed to turmeric are usually credited to curcuminoids, which include curcumin.

Sources: [1]

### Why products are hard to compare

_section: What turmeric and curcumin are · not reviewed_

The NIH page says oral curcumin products vary in how much curcumin they actually contain, and often add substances from other plants, such as piperine from black pepper, which is one way to raise how much curcumin the body absorbs. This site compares products by the curcuminoids stated on the label and doesn’t adjust for absorption.

Sources: [1]

### Knee osteoarthritis (taken by mouth)

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says several meta-analyses have looked at oral turmeric or curcumin for knee pain, stiffness and joint function, and the initial evidence is positive, but higher-quality evidence is needed for definite conclusions, and more research is needed on how absorption affects the results. It says it’s unclear whether curcumin ointment on the skin helps knee pain.

Sources: [1]

### Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says initial research suggests oral turmeric or curcumin might improve some measures of fatty liver disease, but it’s unclear which measures improve consistently.

Sources: [1]

### Mouth sores from cancer treatment

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says initial research suggests turmeric or curcumin taken by mouth or as a mouthwash might improve oral mucositis caused by cancer treatment.

Sources: [1]

### Other conditions

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

The NIH page says there isn’t enough evidence to conclude whether oral turmeric or curcumin helps other health conditions. It lists depression, allergies, itching and high cholesterol among the uses turmeric is promoted for, without a finding for them.

Sources: [1]

### Liver injury with high-absorption products

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The NIH page says highly bioavailable curcumin formulations may harm the liver, and liver damage has been reported in some people who took them. It says to stop and talk with a health care provider at signs such as fatigue, nausea, poor appetite, dark urine or yellowing skin. Each product on this site shows its formulation.

Sources: [1]

### Ordinary products and side effects

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The NIH page says conventionally formulated oral turmeric or curcumin is likely safe in recommended amounts for up to 2 or 3 months, and can cause nausea, vomiting, acid reflux, stomach upset, diarrhea or constipation.

Sources: [1]

### Pregnancy, breastfeeding and medicines

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The NIH page says turmeric supplements may be unsafe during pregnancy, little is known about amounts above food levels while breastfeeding, and anyone taking medicine should talk with a health care provider first because some herbs and medicines interact.

Sources: [1]
