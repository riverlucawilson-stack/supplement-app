## Level 1: human meta-analyses and RCTs

### Catching colds: no effect for most people

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

In a Cochrane review, taking vitamin C every day did not reduce how often people in the general community caught colds (risk ratio 0.97, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.00, 10,708 participants). The NIH fact sheet says the same for most people.

Sources: [2] [1]

### Colds under heavy physical stress

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

In five trials with 598 marathon runners, skiers and soldiers in subarctic exercises, daily vitamin C roughly halved the risk of a cold (risk ratio 0.48, 95% CI 0.35 to 0.64).

Sources: [2]

### How long colds last, if taken every day

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · moderate evidence_

People who took vitamin C regularly had slightly shorter colds: 8% shorter in adults (3% to 12%) and 14% shorter in children (7% to 21%), across 31 comparisons and 9,745 cold episodes. Starting vitamin C after symptoms began showed no consistent effect in 7 comparisons.
- Adults: 8% shorter (95% CI 3% to 12%)
- Children: 14% shorter (95% CI 7% to 21%)

Sources: [2] [1]

## Outside the three levels: reviews, position stands, case reports, or design unclear

### Forms of vitamin C

_section: What vitamin C is · not reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet says vitamin C in supplements is usually ascorbic acid, and some products use sodium ascorbate, calcium ascorbate, other mineral ascorbates, or ascorbic acid with bioflavonoids. It says research has not shown that any form is better than the others.

Sources: [1]

### Cancer and heart disease

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

The NIH fact sheet says vitamin C supplements, with or without other antioxidants, don’t seem to protect people from getting cancer, and that scientists aren’t sure whether vitamin C helps protect against cardiovascular disease.

Sources: [1]

### Recommended intake

_section: How much vitamin C · not reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet lists a Recommended Dietary Allowance of 90 mg a day for adult men and 75 mg for adult women, from all sources. People who smoke need 35 mg more a day.
- Adult men: 90 mg/day
- Adult women: 75 mg/day
- Smokers: add 35 mg/day

Sources: [1]

### Upper limit

_section: How much vitamin C · not reviewed_

The upper limit for adults is 2,000 mg a day from food, drinks and supplements combined.

Sources: [1]

### Too much

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

According to the NIH fact sheet, too much vitamin C can cause diarrhea, nausea and stomach cramps. In people with hemochromatosis, high doses could worsen iron overload.

Sources: [1]

### Medicines

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The fact sheet says vitamin C supplements might interact with chemotherapy and radiation therapy, and that in one study vitamin C with other antioxidants reduced the heart-protective effects of a statin taken with niacin.

Sources: [1]
