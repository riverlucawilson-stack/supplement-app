### What it does in the body

_section: What vitamin C is · reviewed_

The NIH Health Professional fact sheet says vitamin C is needed to make collagen, L-carnitine and some neurotransmitters. It is an important antioxidant, plays a role in immune function, and improves absorption of nonheme iron, the kind in plant foods.

Sources: [1]

### Forms of vitamin C

_section: What vitamin C is · reviewed_

Supplements usually contain ascorbic acid. Some use sodium ascorbate, calcium ascorbate, other mineral ascorbates, ascorbic acid with bioflavonoids, or Ester-C. The Health Professional fact sheet says ascorbic acid has equivalent bioavailability to vitamin C in foods, and that the authors of a review comparing forms concluded simple ascorbic acid is the preferred source. The fact sheet doesn’t discuss liposomal vitamin C.

Sources: [2] [1]

### Catching colds: no effect for most people

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · not supported_

The Health Professional fact sheet, describing a Cochrane review, says taking vitamin C regularly did not significantly lower the risk of catching a cold in the general population.

Sources: [1] [2]

### Colds under heavy physical stress

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · limited or early evidence_

In trials of marathon runners, skiers and soldiers doing exercises in subarctic conditions, regular vitamin C at 250 mg to 1 g a day cut the number of colds by 50%.

Sources: [1]

### How long colds last, if taken every day

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · moderate evidence_

Regular vitamin C modestly shortened colds, by 8% in adults and 14% in children. Starting vitamin C after symptoms began did not change how long colds lasted or how bad they were.
- Adults: 8% shorter
- Children: 14% shorter

Sources: [1] [2]

### Cancer

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · not supported_

The Health Professional fact sheet says most randomized trials found vitamin C, alone or with other nutrients, does not affect cancer risk. For example, 500 mg with vitamin E in the Physicians’ Health Study II (about 8 years, men) didn’t lower prostate or total cancer, and 500 mg a day in the Women’s Antioxidant Cardiovascular Study (9.4 years) had no significant effect on cancer rates or cancer deaths.

Sources: [1]

### Heart disease

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · not supported_

Some observational studies link more vitamin C with lower heart disease risk, and a 2008 meta-analysis of 14 cohorts found this for vitamin C from food but not from supplements. In randomized trials, 500 mg a day had no overall effect on cardiovascular events in women (9.4 years) or major cardiovascular events in men (about 8 years). The fact sheet concludes that most trials don’t give convincing evidence that vitamin C supplements protect against cardiovascular disease.

Sources: [1]

### Recommended intake

_section: How much vitamin C · reviewed_

The NIH fact sheets list a Recommended Dietary Allowance of 90 mg a day for adult men and 75 mg for adult women, from all sources; 85 mg in pregnancy and 120 mg while breastfeeding. People who smoke need 35 mg more a day.
- Adult men: 90 mg/day
- Adult women: 75 mg/day
- Smokers: add 35 mg/day

Sources: [2] [1]

### Upper limit

_section: How much vitamin C · reviewed_

The upper limit for adults is 2,000 mg a day from food, drinks and supplements combined.

Sources: [2] [1]

### Too much

_section: Safety · reviewed_

According to the NIH fact sheets, high doses can cause diarrhea, nausea and stomach cramps. In people with hemochromatosis, long-term high doses could worsen iron overload and damage tissue.

Sources: [2] [1]

### Kidney stones

_section: Safety · reviewed_

The Health Professional fact sheet says high intakes can raise oxalate and uric acid in urine, but studies of 30 mg to 10 g a day have had conflicting results. The best evidence that vitamin C adds to kidney stone formation is in people who already have hyperoxaluria (high urine oxalate).

Sources: [1]

### Medicines

_section: Safety · reviewed_

The fact sheets say the safety of vitamin C supplements during chemotherapy and radiation is controversial, so patients should ask their oncologist first. Vitamin C with other antioxidants may blunt the rise in HDL cholesterol from niacin taken with simvastatin.

Sources: [2] [1]
