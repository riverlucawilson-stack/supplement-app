## Level 1: human meta-analyses and RCTs

### Heart failure

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

A 2024 meta-analysis of 33 randomized trials found lower all-cause death and fewer heart-failure hospitalizations with CoQ10 than without it (both rated moderate-quality evidence), along with improvements in symptom class, a blood marker of heart strain, pumping function and a 6-minute walk test (rated moderate to low quality), without major side effects. The NIH page, written earlier, calls the research on heart failure inconclusive.
- Trials pooled: 33 RCTs
- All-cause death: RR 0.64 (0.48–0.85)
- Heart-failure hospitalization: RR 0.50 (0.37–0.67)

Sources: [2] [1]

### Muscle pain from statins

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

A 2025 meta-analysis of 7 randomized trials (389 people taking statins, 100 to 600 mg CoQ10 a day for 30 to 90 days) found less muscle pain overall; four trials found a reduction and three found none, and the authors say more research is needed before making recommendations. The NIH page says the overall evidence does not support CoQ10 reducing statin muscle pain.
- Trials pooled: 7 RCTs, 389 people
- Doses studied: 100–600 mg/day

Sources: [3] [1]

### Statin muscle pain trials

_section: Doses used in research · not reviewed_

The trials in the 2025 statin review used 100 to 600 mg of CoQ10 a day for 30 to 90 days.
- Daily dose: 100–600 mg
- Duration: 30–90 days

Sources: [3]

### Side effects

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The NIH page says no serious side effects of CoQ10 have been reported, and mild side effects such as insomnia or digestive upsets may occur. The 2024 heart-failure review reported no major adverse effects.

Sources: [1] [2]

## Outside the three levels: reviews, position stands, case reports, or design unclear

### A substance the body makes

_section: What CoQ10 is · not reviewed_

The NIH’s complementary health center describes CoQ10 as a substance naturally present in the human body, with the highest levels in the heart, liver, kidneys and pancreas, and sold in the US as a dietary supplement.

Sources: [1]

### Complications of heart surgery

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says there is evidence that CoQ10 may reduce the risk of some complications of heart surgery.

Sources: [1]

### Heart damage from one chemotherapy drug

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says CoQ10 may reduce the risk of heart damage caused by one type of cancer chemotherapy drug.

Sources: [1]

### Preventing heart disease

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says only a few studies have looked at this and their results are inconclusive.

Sources: [1]

### Other conditions

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says CoQ10 has been studied for ALS, Down syndrome, Huntington’s disease and male infertility, but the research is too limited for any conclusions.

Sources: [1]

### Blood pressure

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

The NIH page says the small amount of evidence available suggests CoQ10 probably doesn’t have a meaningful effect on blood pressure.

Sources: [1]

### Parkinson’s disease

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

The NIH page says a major NIH-funded study found CoQ10, even at higher-than-usual doses, didn’t improve symptoms in early Parkinson’s disease, and a 2017 evaluation of that and smaller studies concluded it isn’t helpful for Parkinson’s symptoms.

Sources: [1]

### Cancer treatment

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

The NIH page says CoQ10 has not been shown to be of value in treating cancer.

Sources: [1]

### Medicine interactions

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The NIH page says CoQ10 may interact with the blood thinner warfarin and with insulin, and may not be compatible with some types of cancer treatment.

Sources: [1]
