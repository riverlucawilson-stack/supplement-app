## Level 1: human meta-analyses and RCTs

### Muscle and strength gains with resistance training

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · moderate evidence_

A meta-analysis of 49 randomized trials (1,863 healthy adults doing resistance training for at least 6 weeks) found protein supplements added small extra gains: 2.49 kg more on a one-rep max, 0.30 kg more fat-free mass, and larger muscle fibers. The extra gain in fat-free mass was smaller in older people and larger in people already trained.
- One-rep max: +2.49 kg (95% CI 0.64 to 4.33)
- Fat-free mass: +0.30 kg (95% CI 0.09 to 0.52)

Sources: [1]

### Benefit levels off around 1.6 g/kg/day

_section: How much protein · reviewed_

In the same meta-analysis, supplementing beyond a total protein intake of about 1.62 g per kg of body weight per day gave no further gain in fat-free mass. Total intake counts food and powder together.

Sources: [1]

## Outside the three levels: reviews, position stands, case reports, or design unclear

### Amounts for athletes

_section: How much protein · reviewed_

The NIH exercise fact sheet says athletes need about 1.2 to 2.0 g of protein per kg of body weight a day, from food and supplements combined.
- Athletes: 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day

Sources: [2]

### At recommended intakes

_section: Safety and contaminants · reviewed_

The NIH exercise fact sheet reports no safety concerns at the daily intakes recommended for athletes, up to about 2.0 g per kg of body weight, and lists no known side effects. It doesn’t cover contaminants such as heavy metals, so we don’t make claims about them here; each product page shows whatever independent testing we could find.
