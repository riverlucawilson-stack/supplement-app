## Outside the three levels: reviews, position stands, case reports, or design unclear

### Live microorganisms, and the strain matters

_section: What probiotics are · not reviewed_

The NIH page describes probiotics as live microorganisms intended to have health benefits. It says different types may have different effects: if one kind of Lactobacillus helps prevent an illness, another Lactobacillus or any Bifidobacterium won’t necessarily do the same. It adds that in most cases we still don’t know which probiotics help, how much to take, or who would benefit most.

Sources: [1]

### C. difficile diarrhea while taking antibiotics

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · moderate evidence_

The NIH page says it is moderately certain that probiotics can reduce the risk of C. difficile diarrhea in adults and children taking antibiotics, but the best strain, dose and duration are uncertain.

Sources: [1]

### Antibiotic-associated diarrhea

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · moderate evidence_

The NIH page says a 2017 review of 17 studies linked probiotics taken with antibiotics to about half the likelihood of antibiotic-associated diarrhea, a tentative conclusion because study quality was moderate. A benefit hasn’t been shown in elderly people; in children it found moderate-quality evidence of protection.

Sources: [1]

### Necrotizing enterocolitis and sepsis in premature infants

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · moderate evidence_

The NIH page says reviews found probiotics helped prevent necrotizing enterocolitis in very-low-birth-weight infants (though not every study showed a benefit) and reduced the risk of sepsis in premature infants. It also says the FDA warned in 2023 that premature infants given probiotics are at risk of severe, potentially fatal infections. This is a hospital decision, not a supplement purchase.

Sources: [1]

### Ulcerative colitis

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says a 2014 review indicated adding probiotics to conventional treatment could help bring on or keep remission. It found no evidence of benefit for Crohn’s disease.

Sources: [1]

### Infant colic (one strain)

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says a 2018 review linked Lactobacillus reuteri DSM 17938 to successful colic treatment, mainly in exclusively breastfed infants.

Sources: [1]

### Irritable bowel syndrome

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says a 2018 review of 53 studies concluded probiotics may help overall IBS symptoms and abdominal pain, but definite conclusions weren’t possible.

Sources: [1]

### Other conditions with some early evidence

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page describes some or low-quality evidence for constipation in adults (especially Bifidobacterium lactis), periodontal disease, allergic rhinitis, hepatic encephalopathy, eczema prevention in infants, and fewer and shorter upper respiratory infections.

Sources: [1]

### Traveler’s diarrhea

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH page says a 2018 review found they may help but didn’t assess study quality, and a 2017 guideline found insufficient evidence to recommend probiotics to prevent or treat traveler’s diarrhea.

Sources: [1]

### Inconclusive or not supported

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

The NIH page says reviews found no beneficial effect for preventing urinary tract infections, no evidence of help for constipation in children or for Crohn’s disease, no consistent benefit for treating eczema, and inconclusive results for asthma, acne, dental cavities, diverticular disease and diarrhea from cancer treatment.

Sources: [1]

### Who should be careful

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The NIH page says probiotics have a long history of apparently safe use in healthy people, but few studies have looked at safety in detail. The risk of harm is greater for people with severe illnesses or weakened immune systems, who should be monitored closely, and for premature infants.

Sources: [1]

### Possible harms

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The NIH page lists infections, harmful substances made by the microorganisms, and transfer of antibiotic-resistance genes to other gut microorganisms as possible harms.

Sources: [1]

### What’s in the bottle

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The NIH page says some probiotic products have been found to contain microorganisms other than those on the label, and in some cases these contaminants may pose serious health risks.

Sources: [1]
