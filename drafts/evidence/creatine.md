## Level 1: human meta-analyses and RCTs

### Memory and reasoning

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

A review of 6 randomized trials (281 people) found creatine may improve short-term memory and reasoning in healthy people. Results for other kinds of thinking were mixed, young adults showed no change, and vegetarians responded better on memory tasks than meat-eaters.

Sources: [3]

### Hair loss

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

In a 12-week randomized trial of resistance-trained men taking 5 g a day, creatine did not change DHT, the DHT-to-testosterone ratio, hair density or hair thickness compared with placebo.

Sources: [4]

### Hormones and hair

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

12 weeks of 5 g a day did not change DHT or hair measures compared with placebo in resistance-trained men.

Sources: [4]

### “Creatine makes your hair fall out”

_section: Common myths · not reviewed_

The one trial that measured hair directly found no difference from placebo in hair density, thickness or DHT after 12 weeks.

Sources: [4]

## Outside the three levels: reviews, position stands, case reports, or design unclear

### It raises the creatine stored in muscle

_section: How creatine works · not reviewed_

Taking 5 g of creatine monohydrate 4 to 6 times a day for 2 or more days raised total creatine in the thigh muscle of 17 people. The rise was biggest in those who started lowest, up to 50% in some. At least 20% of the creatine taken up was stored as phosphocreatine.

Sources: [1]

### Food only partly fills those stores

_section: How creatine works · not reviewed_

A normal diet with 1 to 2 g of creatine a day leaves muscle stores about 60 to 80% full. Supplementing raises muscle creatine and phosphocreatine by 20 to 40%.

Sources: [2]

### Fuller stores help short, hard efforts

_section: How creatine works · not reviewed_

After loading, performance in high-intensity or repeated efforts generally improves by 10 to 20%, depending on how much phosphocreatine rises.

Sources: [2]

### High-intensity and repeated efforts

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · strong evidence_

The ISSN position stand calls creatine monohydrate the most effective supplement available to athletes for increasing high-intensity exercise capacity. Performance in repeated, high-intensity efforts generally improves 10 to 20% after loading.

Sources: [2]

### Muscle mass and strength, alongside training

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · strong evidence_

The same review lists greater gains in muscle mass and strength during training as a benefit, and calls creatine the most effective supplement for increasing lean body mass during training.

Sources: [2]

### Sports beyond the weight room

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · moderate evidence_

The position stand lists sports that may benefit, ranging from sprints, team sports such as soccer, basketball and hockey, and combat sports to 400 to 800 m events and endurance interval training.

Sources: [2]

### Recovery after hard training

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

Individual studies cited in the position stand found better strength during recovery from muscle-damaging exercise, and lower blood markers of muscle damage, in people taking creatine.

Sources: [2]

### Medical conditions

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

Researchers have studied creatine in muscular dystrophy, Parkinson’s and Huntington’s disease, diabetes, osteoarthritis, fibromyalgia, aging, and adolescent depression, among others. The position stand lists these as clinical uses that have been studied.

Sources: [2]

### Women may see smaller strength gains

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The position stand notes that some studies suggest women may not gain as much strength or muscle mass in training with creatine as men.

Sources: [2]

### Loading, then maintenance (fastest)

_section: Doses and protocols used in research · not reviewed_

Described by the ISSN as the most effective way to raise muscle creatine. In one study, 20 g a day for 6 days raised muscle creatine by about 20%.
- Loading dose: 5 g × 4 a day
- By body weight: ~0.3 g/kg/day
- Loading length: 5–7 days
- Then: 3–5 g/day

Sources: [2] [5]

### Steady daily dose (no loading)

_section: Doses and protocols used in research · not reviewed_

Taking 3 g a day raised muscle creatine by a similar 20%, but gradually over 28 days. Until stores are full, it may have less effect on performance than loading.
- Daily dose: 3 g/day
- Time to full stores: ~28 days

Sources: [5] [2]

### Commonly recommended amount

_section: Doses and protocols used in research · not reviewed_

A 2021 expert review describes creatine as well tolerated, especially at recommended doses of 3 to 5 g a day or 0.1 g per kg of body weight a day.
- Daily dose: 3–5 g/day
- By body weight: 0.1 g/kg/day

Sources: [6]

### Larger athletes

_section: Doses and protocols used in research · not reviewed_

Most people maintain full stores with 3 to 5 g a day, but some studies suggest larger athletes may need 5 to 10 g a day.

Sources: [2]

### Taking it with food

_section: Doses and protocols used in research · not reviewed_

Taking creatine with carbohydrate, or carbohydrate and protein, has been reported to lead to more consistent creatine retention in muscle.

Sources: [2]

### When you stop

_section: Doses and protocols used in research · not reviewed_

After stopping, muscle creatine gradually fell and was back to its starting level within 30 days. A 2 g daily dose was enough to hold the raised level for 30 days.

Sources: [5]

### Vegetarians

_section: Doses and protocols used in research · not reviewed_

Vegetarians have lower creatine stores in muscle, so they may see larger increases from supplementing.

Sources: [2]

### Long-term use

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The ISSN position stand concludes that short- and long-term use, up to 30 g a day for 5 years, has been safe and well tolerated in healthy people and in several patient groups from infants to older adults.

Sources: [2]

### At usual doses

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

A 2021 expert review describes creatine as relatively well tolerated, especially at 3 to 5 g a day or 0.1 g per kg a day.

Sources: [6]

### “You have to do a loading phase”

_section: Common myths · not reviewed_

Loading fills muscle stores in about a week. Taking 3 g a day reached a similar level in about 4 weeks without loading.

Sources: [5] [2]

### “It’s only for bodybuilders”

_section: Common myths · not reviewed_

The research lists many sports that may benefit, including team sports, combat sports, middle-distance running and swimming, and endurance interval training.

Sources: [2]
