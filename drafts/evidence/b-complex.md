### Folate and folic acid

_section: What’s in a B-complex · not reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet says the body needs folate to make DNA and for cells to divide. Supplements, including B-complexes, usually contain folic acid, though methylfolate (5-MTHF) is also used. Labels give folate in mcg DFE because the body absorbs more folic acid than food folate: 240 mcg of folic acid equals 400 mcg DFE.

Sources: [1]

### Vitamin B6

_section: What’s in a B-complex · not reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet says the body needs vitamin B6 for more than 100 enzyme reactions in metabolism. In supplements it is usually pyridoxine.

Sources: [2]

### Neural tube defects (folic acid before and in early pregnancy)

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · strong evidence_

The NIH fact sheet says taking folic acid before and during early pregnancy helps prevent neural tube defects, and that all women and teen girls who could become pregnant should get 400 mcg of folic acid daily from supplements, fortified foods or both. It specifies folic acid rather than 5-MTHF for this, even for people with the MTHFR C677T gene variant.
- Amount advised: 400 mcg folic acid/day

Sources: [1]

### Nausea and vomiting in pregnancy (vitamin B6)

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · moderate evidence_

The NIH fact sheet says that, based on several studies, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends vitamin B6 supplements under a doctor’s care for nausea and vomiting during pregnancy.

Sources: [2]

### Stroke (folic acid with other B vitamins)

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The folate fact sheet says some studies have shown that folic acid combined with other B vitamins helps prevent stroke. The B6 fact sheet says B vitamin supplements lower blood homocysteine but do not reduce the risk or severity of heart disease or stroke. The two sheets don’t fully agree.

Sources: [1] [2]

### PMS (vitamin B6)

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH fact sheet says scientists aren’t yet certain, but some studies show vitamin B6 supplements could reduce PMS symptoms.

Sources: [2]

### Depression (folate)

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH fact sheet says folate supplements, particularly with 5-MTHF, might make antidepressants more effective, but whether they help people with normal folate levels isn’t clear.

Sources: [1]

### Heart disease

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

Both NIH fact sheets say the supplements lower homocysteine but don’t directly reduce the risk of heart disease.

Sources: [1] [2]

### Memory and dementia

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

The NIH fact sheets say folic acid with or without other B vitamins doesn’t seem to improve cognitive function or prevent dementia, and vitamin B6 (alone or with B12 and/or folic acid) doesn’t seem to improve cognitive function or mood in healthy people or people with dementia.

Sources: [1] [2]

### Cancer prevention (vitamin B6)

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

The NIH fact sheet says studies have not shown that vitamin B6 supplements help prevent cancer.

Sources: [2]

### Vitamin B6: nerve damage at high doses

_section: Safety and upper limits · not reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet says taking high amounts of vitamin B6 from supplements for a year or longer can cause severe nerve damage, with loss of control of bodily movements, which usually stops when the supplements stop. The US adult upper limit is 100 mg a day from all sources. It notes that in 2023 the European Food Safety Authority set a lower adult limit of 12 mg a day.
- US adult upper limit: 100 mg/day (all sources)
- EU adult upper limit (2023): 12 mg/day

Sources: [2]

### Folic acid: upper limit and hiding B12 deficiency

_section: Safety and upper limits · not reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet sets the adult upper limit for folate from supplements and fortified foods at 1,000 mcg a day. It says large amounts might hide a vitamin B12 deficiency by correcting its anemia but not its nerve damage, and high doses might increase the risk of colorectal and possibly other cancers in some people.
- Adult upper limit: 1,000 mcg/day (supplements and fortified foods)

Sources: [1]

### Medicine interactions

_section: Safety and upper limits · not reviewed_

The NIH fact sheets name interactions between folate and methotrexate, some anti-seizure medicines (phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate) and sulfasalazine, and between vitamin B6 and cycloserine, some epilepsy drugs and theophylline.

Sources: [1] [2]
