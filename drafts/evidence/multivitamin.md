### No standard formula

_section: What a multivitamin is · reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet says multivitamin/mineral supplements combine vitamins and minerals, sometimes with other ingredients, and that there is no standard product or standard list of ingredients. That is why this site compares them by cost per daily serving and lists the nutrient count and forms beside it, rather than by one nutrient.

Sources: [1]

### Reading %DV on the label

_section: What a multivitamin is · reviewed_

The FDA says the %DV is how much one serving contributes to your daily diet, compared with the Daily Value, the recommended amount to eat (or not exceed) each day. As a general guide, 5% DV or less per serving is low and 20% DV or more is high.
- Low: 5% DV or less per serving
- High: 20% DV or more per serving

Sources: [2]

### Death, heart disease and stroke: no clear effect

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · not supported_

The USPSTF’s 2022 review found no link between multivitamins and death from any cause (odds ratio 0.94, 9 trials, 51,550 people), cardiovascular death (0.94, 4 trials, 37,400 people) or cancer death (0.94, 4 trials). The NIH fact sheet says most placebo-controlled studies found no drop in heart attacks, strokes or cardiovascular deaths.
- Death, any cause: OR 0.94 (95% CI 0.87 to 1.01)
- Cardiovascular death: OR 0.94 (95% CI 0.83 to 1.06)

Sources: [3] [1]

### Getting cancer

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The USPSTF found a small drop in new cancers (odds ratio 0.93, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.99, 4 trials, 48,859 people), but it did not match the cancer death results. It notes the largest trial included only male physicians and another followed people for a median of only 3.6 years. The NIH fact sheet says overall it is unclear whether multivitamins affect cancer risk.

Sources: [3] [1]

### Memory and thinking in older adults

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH Health Professional fact sheet describes three studies within the COSMOS trial of older adults (mean ages 69 to 73). In COSMOS-Mind (2,262 people, 3 years) a daily multivitamin improved global cognition, most in people with a history of cardiovascular disease. In COSMOS-Web (3,562 people) memory was better at 1 year and across 3 years. In COSMOS-Clinic (573 people) episodic memory improved over 2 years, but executive function and attention did not. A meta-analysis of the three (more than 5,000 people) found improved global cognition and episodic memory. The earlier Physicians’ Health Study II found no effect on cognitive decline.

Sources: [4]

### Eye disease (AREDS formula)

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed · moderate evidence_

AREDS is a high-dose eye formula, not a general multivitamin. In 4,757 people aged 55 to 80 followed for about 6.3 years, daily vitamin C (500 mg), vitamin E (400 IU), beta-carotene (15 mg), zinc (80 mg) and copper (2 mg) cut the risk of going from intermediate to advanced age-related macular degeneration by 28% and vision loss by 27%. It did not prevent macular degeneration from starting or change cataract risk. AREDS2 (4,203 people) found adding omega-3s or lutein/zeaxanthin gave no further benefit, and beta-carotene was not a required ingredient.

Sources: [4]

### Official recommendation

_section: What it can and can’t help with · reviewed_

For healthy, non-pregnant adults, the USPSTF (June 2022) says the evidence is insufficient to weigh the benefits and harms of multivitamins for preventing cardiovascular disease or cancer (an “I” statement). It recommends against beta-carotene and vitamin E supplements for that purpose.

Sources: [3]

### Pregnancy and folic acid

_section: Who might need specific nutrients · reviewed_

The USPSTF recommends that anyone planning or able to become pregnant take 400 to 800 mcg of folic acid a day. The NIH fact sheet says a prenatal multivitamin helps cover folic acid, iron, iodine and vitamin D.

Sources: [3] [1]

### Over 50 and vitamin B12

_section: Who might need specific nutrients · reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet says people over 50 should get B12 from fortified foods or supplements, because the body might not absorb enough of the B12 in food.

Sources: [1]

### Restricted diets

_section: Who might need specific nutrients · reviewed_

The fact sheet says people who don’t get enough nutrients from food, eat very low-calorie diets, have a poor appetite or avoid certain foods (such as vegans) might consider a multivitamin.

Sources: [1]

### Basic multivitamins and upper limits

_section: Safety · reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet says a basic multivitamin is unlikely to harm your health, but adding fortified foods and other supplements can push some nutrients over their upper limits. Products above recommended amounts raise the risk of too much iron, vitamin A, zinc, niacin and folic acid.

Sources: [1]

### Vitamin A in pregnancy

_section: Safety · reviewed_

The Health Professional fact sheet says too much preformed vitamin A (retinol and similar forms, but not beta-carotene) in pregnancy can raise the risk of birth defects. The upper limit in pregnancy is 3,000 mcg a day for women and 2,800 mcg for adolescents. Prenatal supplements generally provide no vitamin A as retinol.

Sources: [4]

### Smokers and beta-carotene

_section: Safety · reviewed_

The fact sheet says smokers, and perhaps former smokers, should avoid multivitamins with large amounts of beta-carotene or vitamin A. In one trial of 29,133 male smokers, 20 mg of beta-carotene a day was followed by 18% more lung cancer over 5 to 8 years. In another of 18,314 smokers, former smokers and asbestos-exposed workers, beta-carotene with vitamin A raised lung cancer risk by 28% after about 4 years.

Sources: [1] [4] [3]

### Warfarin and vitamin K

_section: Safety · reviewed_

The fact sheet says basic multivitamins don’t usually interact with medicines, except that people on warfarin should talk to their provider before taking one with vitamin K.

Sources: [1]
