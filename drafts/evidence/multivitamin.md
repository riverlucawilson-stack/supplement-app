### No standard formula

_section: What a multivitamin is · not reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet says multivitamin/mineral supplements combine vitamins and minerals, sometimes with other ingredients, and that there is no standard product or standard list of ingredients. That is why this site compares them by cost per daily serving and lists the nutrient count and forms beside it, rather than by one nutrient.

Sources: [1]

### Death, heart disease and stroke: no clear effect

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · not supported_

The USPSTF’s 2022 review found no link between multivitamins and death from any cause (odds ratio 0.94, 9 trials, 51,550 people), cardiovascular death (0.94, 4 trials, 37,400 people) or cancer death (0.94, 4 trials). The NIH fact sheet says most placebo-controlled studies found no drop in heart attacks, strokes or cardiovascular deaths.
- Death, any cause: OR 0.94 (95% CI 0.87 to 1.01)
- Cardiovascular death: OR 0.94 (95% CI 0.83 to 1.06)

Sources: [2] [1]

### Getting cancer

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The USPSTF found a small drop in new cancers (odds ratio 0.93, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.99, 4 trials, 48,859 people), but it did not match the cancer death results. It notes the largest trial included only male physicians and another followed people for a median of only 3.6 years. The NIH fact sheet says overall it is unclear whether multivitamins affect cancer risk.

Sources: [2] [1]

### Memory and thinking in older adults

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed · limited or early evidence_

The NIH fact sheet says a few studies found that older people who took a multivitamin for 1 to 3 years had better cognitive function and memory. It does not name the studies.

Sources: [1]

### Official recommendation

_section: What it can and can’t help with · not reviewed_

For healthy, non-pregnant adults, the USPSTF (June 2022) says the evidence is insufficient to weigh the benefits and harms of multivitamins for preventing cardiovascular disease or cancer (an “I” statement). It recommends against beta-carotene and vitamin E supplements for that purpose.

Sources: [2]

### Pregnancy and folic acid

_section: Who might need specific nutrients · not reviewed_

The USPSTF recommends that anyone planning or able to become pregnant take 400 to 800 mcg of folic acid a day. The NIH fact sheet says a prenatal multivitamin helps cover folic acid, iron, iodine and vitamin D.

Sources: [2] [1]

### Over 50 and vitamin B12

_section: Who might need specific nutrients · not reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet says people over 50 should get B12 from fortified foods or supplements, because the body might not absorb enough of the B12 in food.

Sources: [1]

### Restricted diets

_section: Who might need specific nutrients · not reviewed_

The fact sheet says people who don’t get enough nutrients from food, eat very low-calorie diets, have a poor appetite or avoid certain foods (such as vegans) might consider a multivitamin.

Sources: [1]

### Basic multivitamins and upper limits

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The NIH fact sheet says a basic multivitamin is unlikely to harm your health, but adding fortified foods and other supplements can push some nutrients over their upper limits. Products above recommended amounts raise the risk of too much iron, vitamin A, zinc, niacin and folic acid.

Sources: [1]

### Smokers and beta-carotene

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The fact sheet says smokers, and perhaps former smokers, should avoid multivitamins with large amounts of beta-carotene or vitamin A. The USPSTF reports two trials in smokers or asbestos-exposed workers where beta-carotene raised lung cancer risk.

Sources: [1] [2]

### Warfarin and vitamin K

_section: Safety · not reviewed_

The fact sheet says basic multivitamins don’t usually interact with medicines, except that people on warfarin should talk to their provider before taking one with vitamin K.

Sources: [1]
